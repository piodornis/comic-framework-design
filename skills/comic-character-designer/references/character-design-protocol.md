## Goal

Build a character design that is recognizable, repeatable, story-compatible, style-compatible, and usable by later production workflows.

Do not optimize only for one attractive hero image. Prefer a design that can survive different poses, expressions, angles, lighting conditions, and repeated appearances.

## Design from narrative constraints

Read the story-side character profile and only the world/story sources that materially affect appearance.

Separate:

- factual visual constraints already in canon
- logical visual consequences that are `INFERENCE`
- genuinely new visual inventions that are `PROPOSAL`

Examples:

- established occupation may constrain equipment or wear patterns
- environment may constrain protective clothing or materials
- social role may influence formality, maintenance quality, or visual access to resources
- personality may inform posture or expression but should not be reduced to a visual stereotype

Do not force every narrative trait into an obvious visual symbol.

## Identity anchors

Define three classes of traits.

### Fixed anchors

Traits that must remain stable unless explicitly changed.

Examples:

- body silhouette
- facial geometry or display layout
- distinctive markings
- signature color placement
- characteristic costume structure
- recurring equipment

### Controlled variables

Traits that may vary within a known system.

Examples:

- expression
- pose
- lighting response
- approved costume variants
- detachable modules
- local wear or dirt

### Open variables

Traits not yet decided.

Keep them visibly open rather than allowing each new image to invent a different answer.

## Silhouette and proportions

Prioritize immediate recognition at small scale.

Check:

- head/body ratio
- shoulder or upper-body width
- limb length and mass
- hand/foot size
- center of gravity
- negative space between major forms
- recurring asymmetry

Use shape hierarchy rather than surface detail to create identity.

## Face and expression system

Define how emotion is communicated.

For human or humanoid characters, consider:

- eye shape and openness
- brow behavior
- mouth geometry
- cheek and jaw movement
- hair or head-shape contribution

For robots, masks, or non-human characters, define the equivalent signal system:

- display eyes
- light states
- panel movement
- antenna posture
- ear or fin position
- body tilt
- hand or manipulator posture

Keep the expression system compatible with the active Style Pack.

## Costume, shell, and equipment

Design recurring elements as systems rather than decoration.

For each recurring item, define:

- purpose
- attachment or wearing logic
- approximate scale
- construction
- key shape
- color/material relationship
- whether it is always present, optional, or scene-specific

Avoid adding equipment that implies unsupported narrative facts.

## Palette and materials

Use the Style Pack's palette behavior first, then define character-specific color allocation.

Document:

- base colors
- accent colors
- identifier colors
- material differences
- wear behavior
- emissive elements
- allowable local variation

Prefer color placement that reinforces recognition even when lighting changes.

## Style integration

Apply the active Style Pack to the character without copying project-external reference content.

Translate style rules into character-specific consequences:

- contour hierarchy
- simplification level
- facial abstraction
- shadow geometry
- material rendering
- texture density
- detail density

If the Style Pack is missing or contradictory, report the gap and defer overall art-direction decisions to Comic Style Director.

## Variants

Create variants around a stable core.

A useful variant should test a named question such as:

- warmer vs cooler palette
- heavier vs lighter silhouette
- formal vs work costume
- clean vs worn state
- compact vs extended equipment

Do not create arbitrary alternatives that change several identity dimensions at once unless broad exploration is explicitly requested.

## Turnarounds and sheets

Use consistent proportions across views.

Before producing a turnaround, identify:

- stable front-view anchors
- side-depth relationships
- rear construction
- hidden attachments
- asymmetrical elements
- scale markers

For expression or pose sheets, preserve construction while changing only the intended variable.

## Continuity and repeatability

Record continuity anchors that later production must preserve.

Examples:

- exact side for scars, badges, pouches, antennae, or damage
- recurring color placement
- number and placement of mechanical joints
- costume closures
- hand or manipulator construction
- fixed height relationships
- equipment attachment points

If repeated generation exposes unstable details, simplify or formalize them rather than accepting uncontrolled drift.

## Conflict handling

If design sources disagree:

1. identify the conflicting traits
2. rank sources by project priority
3. preserve locked and approved anchors
4. mark unresolved issues as `CONFLICT`
5. create separate alternatives only when useful
6. ask for a creator decision when the difference affects identity or canon

Do not blend contradictory references silently.
